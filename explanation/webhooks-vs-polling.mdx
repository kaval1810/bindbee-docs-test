---
title: "Webhooks vs polling"
description: "What a webhook tells you, what it doesn't, and when polling is the better instrument."
---

Bindbee can notify you when a sync finishes and when records change. It is tempting to treat this as a real-time data feed and build on it accordingly. That expectation produces a system that mostly works and occasionally loses data, which is the worst kind.

## A webhook is a signal, not a delivery

Two things arrive by webhook: sync-status events telling you a connector's sync completed or failed, and data-change events telling you records were created or modified.

Neither carries the data. A change event tells you something happened and gives you enough to go and read it. Your handler's job is to fetch, not to consume a payload.

This is a deliberate constraint rather than an omission, and it removes an entire class of bug. If a webhook carried the record, that record would be a snapshot from the moment the event fired, and by the time your handler processed it the value might already have moved. Fetching on receipt means you always act on current state.

<Info>
Sync-status events are the more useful of the two and the more overlooked. They are the only reliable way to know an initial sync has finished, and the only push signal that a connector has stopped working.
</Info>

## What webhooks cannot tell you

**That nothing happened.** Silence is ambiguous. No events could mean no changes, or a delivery failure, or a connector that broke three weeks ago. A system that only reacts to events cannot distinguish these.

**That you received everything.** Webhook delivery over the public internet is best-effort. Endpoints go down, deploys drop requests, networks fail. Any design that assumes every event arrives will eventually be wrong.

**Fine-grained change detail.** An event tells you a record changed, not which field or what it was before. If you need before-and-after, you need your own copy to compare against.

**Exactly-once delivery.** A failed delivery is retried up to four times within 60 seconds, with a 10-second timeout per attempt, and every non-`2xx` counts as a failure. That means duplicates are normal and your handler has to be idempotent. It also means an outage longer than a minute loses the event permanently - retries stop, and nothing is redelivered later. See [Recover a Missed Webhook Delivery](/how-to/reliability/recover-a-missed-delivery).

## When to poll instead

Polling is not the primitive option. For several jobs it is the correct one.

**Reconciliation.** A periodic full read that compares against your copy is how you catch what webhooks dropped. Even a well-built event pipeline benefits from a nightly sweep.

**Bulk operations.** Payroll cycles, benefits open enrolment, and reporting runs want a consistent view at a moment in time, not a stream of individual changes.

**Detecting absence.** Records that vanish, connectors that go quiet, syncs that stopped running - these are all non-events, and only a scheduled check finds them.

**Initial load.** Reading everything once is a read, not a stream.

The durable pattern is both: webhooks to react quickly to what you hear about, scheduled reads to catch what you did not. Events reduce latency; polling provides the floor.

## Freshness is bounded by the sync, not the webhook

One expectation worth correcting. A webhook fires when Bindbee's sync detects a change, and syncs run on a schedule measured in hours. The event is prompt relative to the sync, not relative to the customer's action.

An employee updated in the HRIS at 9am does not generate a webhook at 9am. It generates one when the next sync picks the change up. Webhooks remove polling overhead; they do not make data fresher than the sync cadence allows.

## Why it works this way

Signal-not-payload follows from Bindbee holding a synced copy rather than proxying.

If webhooks carried full records, they would become a second, parallel data channel with its own consistency problems - events arriving out of order, a stale payload overwriting a newer read, your copy and Bindbee's disagreeing with no obvious authority between them. Keeping the API the single source of truth means there is always one right answer to what a record currently says.

Best-effort delivery is a property of webhooks in general rather than a Bindbee limitation, and pretending otherwise would encourage exactly the wrong architecture. Being explicit that events can be missed is what pushes you toward the reconciliation sweep that makes the system actually reliable.

## What this means for you

- **Fetch on receipt.** Do not build handlers around event payloads.
- **Run a scheduled reconciliation** regardless of how good your event handling is.
- **Gate first-run logic on sync-status events**, not on data appearing.
- **Make handlers idempotent.** Assume events can repeat or arrive out of order.
- **Do not promise real-time.** Freshness is bounded by sync cadence.
