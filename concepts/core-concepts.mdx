Five words carry most of the weight in Bindbee's API, and three of them sound interchangeable. Getting them straight early saves a great deal of confusion later, particularly when reading error messages or configuring custom fields, where the difference between an integration and a connector determines what your change affects.

## The five levels

**Category** is the kind of system: HRIS, ATS, or LMS. Categories determine which models exist. Employees and payroll runs belong to HRIS; candidates and offers belong to ATS. A category appears in endpoint paths and in custom field configuration.

**Integration** is a specific vendor system - BambooHR, Workday, Greenhouse. An integration belongs to a category and defines what data is available and how it is retrieved. Integrations are identified by a slug, and that slug is what you use when configuring something that should apply to every customer on that system.

**Connector** is one of your customers' authorised connections to one integration. If forty of your customers use BambooHR, you have one BambooHR integration and forty BambooHR connectors. A connector holds the credentials, owns the sync schedule, and is identified by a connector token.

**Model** is a unified object type - employee, employment, benefit, candidate. Models are consistent across every integration in a category, which is the whole point of a unified API.

**Record** is a single instance of a model, synced from one connector.

Reading upward from a record: this employee came from this connector, which is your customer's connection to this integration, which belongs to this category.

<Info>
The integration-versus-connector distinction is the one that matters operationally. Anything scoped to an integration affects every customer on that system. Anything scoped to a connector affects one customer. Custom field mappings use exactly this split, and so does most of what you will configure.
</Info>

## Organisation and environment

Above all of this sits your **organisation** - your company's account. Scope configuration and custom field definitions belong here, which is why they apply across everything below.

Each organisation has two **environments**: Development and Production. They hold separate connectors, keys and logs, so testing against a sandbox tenant never touches live customer data.

What they do not hold separately is configuration. Scope and custom fields are organisation-level, so a change made while testing in Development is a change in Production as well. This surprises people who expect environments to be fully independent, and it is worth internalising before you start configuring.

A connector's environment is settled when it is created, but it is not permanent: it can be switched without the end user reconnecting, though not from the dashboard. [Environments](/concepts/environments/overview) covers what each side holds and what a move does.

## Two tokens

Requests carry two pieces of authentication, and they answer different questions.

Your **API key** identifies your organisation. It is secret, it belongs on your server, and it never reaches a browser.

The **connector token** identifies which customer's data you are asking about. Without it, Bindbee knows who is asking but not whose employees you want.

This is why reading employees is not simply a matter of holding an API key. Every data request needs both - one for authority, one for subject.

## Why it works this way

The layering exists so that configuration can be expressed at the level where it is actually true.

Scope is a decision about your product: which data your application needs at all. That is organisation-level because it does not vary by customer. A custom field mapping is usually a fact about a source system - where BambooHR keeps its cost centre - which makes it integration-level. Occasionally it is a fact about one customer's unusual tenant configuration, which makes it connector-level. Credentials are always about one customer, so they live on the connector and nowhere else.

Flattening these would force a choice between configuring everything once and getting it wrong for the exceptions, or configuring everything per customer and drowning in it. The hierarchy lets each setting sit at its natural scope, with narrower scopes overriding wider ones where they exist.

The two-token split is a security boundary rather than an organisational one. Connector tokens are safe to hand to a specific request path because they grant access to exactly one customer's data. An API key is not, because it grants access to all of it. Keeping them separate means the identifier that travels most widely is the one with the least authority.

## What this means for you

- **Integration-scoped changes affect every customer on that system.** Check the scope before applying a mapping fix.
- **Configuration crosses environments; data does not.** Plan scope before you begin testing, not after.
- **Keep API keys server-side.** Connector tokens identify a customer, not your organisation.
- **When reading an error, note which level it refers to.** A connector problem is one customer; an integration problem is all of them.
