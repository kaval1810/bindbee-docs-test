You expected the employee list to shrink when someone left. It did not. Or it shrank without anyone leaving.

Employment status is where the difference between how HR systems actually work and how applications assume they work shows up most sharply.

## Terminated employees do not disappear

HR systems almost never delete people. A termination sets a status and a date; the record stays, because payroll, benefits continuation, tax reporting, and rehire eligibility all depend on it continuing to exist.

Bindbee follows this. A terminated employee remains in your results with an inactive employment status and a termination date populated. Filtering to current staff is your application's decision, not something the API does on your behalf.

<Info>
The corollary matters more than it sounds: an unfiltered employee count is a lifetime count, not a headcount. If your billing, seat allocation, or reporting is keyed to employee count, it needs to filter on status or it will drift upward permanently.
</Info>

Some source systems have no delete at all and use a flag instead - an "is deleted" marker alongside the active status - so a record that was removed in the customer's UI still arrives, marked. Where that distinction exists, it survives in raw data.

## Custom statuses collapse into a fixed set

Source systems let customers define their own status vocabularies. One tenant runs Active, Active PT, Leave, Inactive. Another runs a dozen values encoding division and contract type alongside employment state.

The unified model has one status enum. Every local vocabulary has to map into it, and values with no clean equivalent land on the generic fallback. A tenant distinguishing full-time from part-time through status will lose that distinction on the unified field - both are simply active.

The original string is preserved in raw data. If the local vocabulary carries meaning your application needs, promote it into a custom field rather than trying to recover it from the enum.

## Transfers and rehires create new records

{/* DIAGRAM (tier 2): before/after for one person. Before - one Employee, one open Employment. After a transfer - the same Employee with one terminated Employment and one open one. After a rehire on a system that mints a new identifier - two Employee records, which is the duplicate this section warns about. Should make clear that "current" is the employment without a termination date, not the first in the array. */}

Two lifecycle events routinely produce a second record for someone who never left.

A **transfer** between positions, departments, or legal entities is implemented in several systems as terminating the existing employment and creating a new one. The person now has one terminated employment and one active employment. Both are real and both are correct - the employment history is genuinely two segments.

A **rehire** is similar, and inconsistent between systems. Some reactivate the original record and clear the termination date. Others create a new record entirely, sometimes with a new identifier. The second case produces a duplicate at the employee level, not just the employment level.

{/* VERIFY: HrisEmployment in spec.json has no termination_date - only effective_date. termination_date and termination_reason are on HrisEmployeeResponse. Either the spec omits the field on employment, or termination is employee-level only and the current-employment selection rule below needs rewriting around effective_date. Confirm with engineering before this ships. */}

Where an employee has multiple employments, the current one is the one without a termination date, or the one with the latest effective start where several are open. Do not assume the first element of the array.

## Disappearing employees

An active employee vanishing from results has a small number of causes, and the order to check them in is:

**Scope or permissions narrowed.** The most common cause and the least dramatic. If the credentials lost access to a subset of workers, those workers stop syncing entirely rather than arriving marked inactive.

**The upstream identifier changed.** The employee is still there under a new identity, and the old record is what went quiet.

**A file-based connector stopped listing them.** For integrations delivered as files rather than API calls, absence from the current file is a meaningful signal and is interpreted as the person no longer being active.

## Why it works this way

Bindbee reports status rather than filtering on it, and that is the decision that generates most of the surprise here.

Filtering by default would make the common case - show me current staff - effortless, and would silently break every other case. Benefits administration needs terminated employees for COBRA continuation. Payroll needs them for final pay and year-end reporting. Compliance needs them for retention periods measured in years. A platform that hid them by default would be unusable for a large share of what it exists to support, and the workaround would be worse than the filter.

The enum collapse is the same trade seen elsewhere in the unified model. A status field that faithfully reproduced every customer's local vocabulary would not be a unified field at all - your code would branch per tenant. Mapping to a fixed set is what makes one code path work, and raw data is what makes the loss recoverable.

## What this means for you

- **Filter on employment status explicitly.** An unfiltered list is everyone who has ever worked there.
- **Do not key headcount or billing to raw record count.**
- **Expect multiple employments per person**, and select the current one by termination date rather than by position in the array.
- **If a customer's local status vocabulary carries meaning you need**, promote it into a custom field. The enum will not preserve it.
- **A disappearing employee is a permissions question first**, an identity question second.
