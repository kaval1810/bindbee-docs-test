Your employee count went up by forty overnight and nobody was hired. Or an employee you have been tracking for months suddenly appears twice, with the same name and different identifiers.

Neither is a bug in the usual sense. Both follow from how Bindbee decides that two things it has seen are the same thing.

## What makes a record the same record

Every synced record carries two identifiers that matter.

**`remote_id`** is the source system's own identifier for that object. Bindbee does not invent it and does not modify it.

**`id`** is Bindbee's identifier. It is stable, it is what you should store, and it is what you use to read the record back.

Identity is resolved per connector. Bindbee treats a record as the one it already holds when the `remote_id` matches within that connector. A new `remote_id` is a new record, always - there is no name matching, no fuzzy comparison, no attempt to recognise that two identifiers describe the same human.

<Info>
This also means the same person appearing under two connectors is two records with two different `id` values. A customer running separate HRIS tenants for two legal entities will have their shared employees duplicated across those connectors, and reconciling them is your application's job.
</Info>

## Why duplicates appear

Three situations produce a second record for someone who already exists.

**The upstream identifier changed.** Some HR systems allow an employee ID to be edited, or reassign identifiers during a migration or a payroll provider switch. From Bindbee's side this is indistinguishable from a new person arriving and the old one going quiet. The original record remains, now stale; the new one syncs alongside it.

**The employee was recreated upstream.** A rehire entered as a new record rather than a reactivation, or a record deleted and re-added to correct an error, produces a new source identifier and therefore a new record.

**A transfer created a new employment.** In several systems, moving someone between positions or entities terminates one record and creates another rather than editing in place. Both persist.

The common thread is that the source system changed its own notion of identity. Bindbee followed it.

## Reconciling when identifiers do not line up

Because Bindbee will not merge records on your behalf, reconciliation has to happen in your application, and it has to use something other than the identifier that changed.

The practical approach is to match on stable personal attributes - work email, date of birth, full name, or a national identifier where you hold one and are permitted to use it. Email is usually the strongest single signal in an HR context; names alone are not sufficient at any meaningful scale.

Two operational habits make this considerably easier. Store Bindbee's `id` as your foreign key rather than the `remote_id`, so an upstream identifier change does not break your own references. And treat a sudden jump in record count as a signal to check for duplicates rather than as growth - a headcount that moves without a corresponding hiring event is the clearest early warning you get.

## Why it works this way

Automatic merging is the obvious feature to want here, and it is the one thing a data platform in this position should not do.

Merging two records means asserting that two humans are the same human, on evidence that is often ambiguous. Names repeat. Personal emails get reused. Contractors get rehired as employees with new records deliberately, because the business needs them separated. A platform that merges on similarity will eventually merge two different people, and the consequence in an HR or payroll context is that one person's compensation, benefits, or bank details surface under another person's identity. That failure is far worse than a duplicate, and it is silent.

Following the source system's identity exactly means Bindbee is never the thing that introduced an error. If the HRIS says these are two records, you get two records, and you retain the ability to decide otherwise using context Bindbee does not have - your own user accounts, your own onboarding history, your own tolerance for a false match.

## What this means for you

- **Store `id`, not `remote_id`.** Bindbee's identifier survives upstream identifier changes; the source system's does not.
- **Build reconciliation into your data layer from the start**, keyed on work email where available. Retrofitting it after duplicates accumulate is materially harder.
- **Treat unexplained count changes as a duplicate signal**, not as growth.
- **The same person under two connectors is two records.** If your customers run multiple tenants, this is a normal state, not an error.
