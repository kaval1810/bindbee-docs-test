A connector is the only object in Bindbee with a lifecycle you have to manage. It is created by someone outside your company, it can stop working without anything on your side changing, and one of the recovery actions available to you destroys data.

## The states

**Pending.** Created when you initiate a connection, before your customer has authorised it. No credentials, no data, no syncs.

**Active.** Authorised and syncing on schedule. The normal steady state.

**Broken.** Credentials that no longer work. This covers several underlying causes that behave identically from your side: an OAuth token that expired and could not refresh, an API key the customer rotated, an integration user that was deactivated, or permissions that were narrowed after authorisation.

A broken connector does not lose the data it already synced. It stops acquiring new data, and everything you have goes stale in place - which is precisely why this failure is easy to miss. Reads keep succeeding.

<Warning>
There is no error on the data endpoints when a connector is broken. Your application will keep reading successfully from a connector that stopped syncing weeks ago. Sync-status webhooks and connector state are the only reliable signals.
</Warning>

## Relinking versus deleting

When a connector breaks, both actions available to you look like they mean "fix it". They do not.

**Relinking** sends your customer back through authorisation to supply working credentials. The connector keeps its identity, its history, and its synced records. Existing data reconciles against what the source system now returns, and normal syncing resumes. This is the correct response to essentially every broken connector.

**Deleting** removes the connector and its data. The customer's identifiers, the records synced under it, the sync history - all of it. Recovering means creating a new connector and running a fresh initial sync, and every record comes back with a new Bindbee `id`, breaking any foreign key you stored.

Deletion is the right action when a customer churns or when a connection was created in error. It is the wrong instinct for a broken one, and it is not reversible.

## Credential rotation is routine

Customers rotate API keys, cycle service accounts, and offboard the administrator who originally authorised the connection. Each of these breaks a connector through no fault of anyone's.

The last one is worth planning for specifically. When authorisation is tied to an individual's account rather than a dedicated integration user, that person leaving the company takes the connection with them. It is one of the more common causes of a connector breaking months after a clean setup, and it tends to be discovered late because nothing announces it.

## What deletion actually removes

Deleting a connector removes the synced records associated with it. This is worth stating plainly because it is sometimes what you want - a churned customer's employee data should not persist indefinitely, and connector deletion is the mechanism for removing it.

It is also why deletion cannot be the reflex for a broken connection. The same operation that satisfies a data-removal request destroys a recoverable integration.

## Why it works this way

Keeping data through a broken state is the decision that produces the silent-staleness problem, and it is still the right one.

The alternative is to make data unavailable the moment credentials fail - reads start erroring, and the problem is impossible to miss. That is louder, and it converts a degradation into an outage. Your application stops working for that customer entirely because their IT department rotated a key, even though the data you hold is minutes old and perfectly usable. For most applications, slightly stale data is enormously better than no data.

Preserving data through relink is what makes recovery cheap. Because the connector keeps its identity, a customer who fixes their credentials gets continuity rather than a new set of identifiers and a broken join in your database. The trade is that failure is quiet, which puts the burden on monitoring rather than on error handling.

## What this means for you

- **Monitor connector state, not read success.** Reads succeed regardless.
- **Relink to fix; delete to remove.** They are not interchangeable, and deletion is not reversible.
- **Encourage dedicated integration users at setup** rather than authorisation tied to an individual's account.
- **Treat connector deletion as your data-removal mechanism** for churned customers.
- **Surface stale connectors to whoever manages the customer relationship**, since the fix is on their side.
