---
title: "Benefits models"
description: "The difference between benefits, employer benefits, dependent benefits and benefit coverages, and when to use each."
---

Five models carry benefits data, and their names are close enough that picking the wrong one is easy. The distinction that matters is not what the benefit is - it is whose fact it is.

## Whose fact is it

**Employer benefit** is the plan itself, as the employer offers it. A medical plan with a carrier, a name, and a plan year. It exists whether or not anyone has enrolled.

**Benefit** is one employee's enrolment in a plan. This is where the employee's contribution amount, coverage tier, and effective dates live. One employee enrolled in medical, dental, and vision has three.

**Dependent benefit** is a dependant's coverage under an employee's enrolment - the enrolled spouse or child, as distinct from the employee's own enrolment.

**Dependent** is the person: name, relationship, date of birth. Independent of any benefit, because a dependant exists whether or not they are covered.

**Benefit coverage** carries coverage detail at a finer grain - amounts and terms tied to a specific coverage target rather than to the enrolment as a whole. This matters for plans like life insurance, where the sum assured differs between the employee, the spouse, and each child under a single enrolment.

The shortest way to choose: employer benefit for what is offered, benefit for who enrolled, dependent benefit for who else is covered, dependent for who they are, benefit coverage for how much.

<Info>
The most common modelling error is looking for coverage amounts on the enrolment. On plans with per-person amounts, the enrolment carries the contribution and the coverage model carries the amounts. Reading only the enrolment produces a plausible, incomplete picture.
</Info>

## Plan years bound everything

Benefits are the most time-bound data in an HR system. A plan year opens, enrolments run against it, contributions are deducted during it, and then the whole structure is replaced at renewal.

Two consequences follow.

**Enrolments accumulate.** An employee in their third year has three medical enrolments, not one that was updated. The current one is bounded by the current plan year. An unfiltered read returns benefits history, and treating it as current state overstates coverage.

**Renewal changes identity.** At renewal, most systems create new plan records rather than editing existing ones. Plans that look identical across years are distinct objects with distinct identifiers.

## Where deductions live

Benefits do not carry the money movement. What was actually withheld from a paycheque lives in payroll - deductions on an employee payroll run.

Connecting them is a real piece of work, because the deduction carries a payroll code and the enrolment carries a plan, and the correspondence between them is defined in the customer's configuration rather than in either record. Reconciling a deduction to a plan year additionally requires the payment date, since a January deduction may belong to either plan year depending on how the customer's calendar falls.

This is the boundary between benefits data and payroll data, and it does not resolve cleanly in either direction.

## Why it works this way

Splitting the plan from the enrolment is what makes the data usable at scale.

Folding them together would put the plan's name, carrier, and terms on every employee's record - duplicated across everyone enrolled, and inconsistent the moment one copy is updated and another is not. Separating them means the plan is stated once and enrolments reference it, so a correction to the plan is a correction everywhere.

The separate coverage model exists because coverage is genuinely not an attribute of the enrolment on many plans. Life insurance with different sums assured for employee, spouse, and children cannot be expressed as fields on a single enrolment record without either fixing the number of covered persons in the schema or nesting arbitrarily. A separate model with its own target is the shape that holds all of it, at the cost of one more read.

Keeping dependants separate from dependant benefits reflects the same principle. A dependant is a person who exists independently of coverage - they may be enrolled in some plans and not others, added mid-year, or recorded without being covered at all.

## What this means for you

- **Filter enrolments by plan year.** Unfiltered reads return history.
- **Read benefit coverages when amounts matter**, not just enrolments.
- **Do not expect plan identifiers to survive renewal.** Match on plan attributes, not identity.
- **Deduction-to-plan reconciliation needs payroll data and payment dates**, and depends on the customer's own code configuration.
- **A dependant may exist without coverage.** The models are separate for a reason.
